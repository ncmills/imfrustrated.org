*This article is general information, not legal advice. Noise rules, limits, and complaint procedures are set locally, so confirm the details with your own city or county.*

## What is a noise nuisance complaint?

A noise nuisance complaint is a written report to the body that enforces noise rules, describing noise that interferes with your home life. It is usually grounded in a local ordinance. Separately, the law recognizes a civil claim: [a private nuisance is a use of property that significantly harms another person's use or enjoyment of their private land](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/private_nuisance), and it affects one or a limited number of individuals rather than the public. Your letter should say which route you are using: asking for ordinance enforcement, or putting the other party on notice of a nuisance.

## Who do I send a noise complaint to?

Send it to your city or county, because that is where noise control is usually written and enforced. In [a peer-reviewed review of 60 U.S. community noise ordinances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9347828/), law enforcement was a designated enforcement authority in 31, health agencies in 10 (16.7%), and only four communities had a dedicated noise control officer. So the right recipient might be the police non-emergency line, a health department, or a code office, depending on where you live.

Check your local government website to find the right office before you send anything.

## How loud is too loud?

It depends on your locality, and some codes also bar "unreasonable" noise with no decibel number. Examples of published residential limits:

- [Montgomery County, MD](https://www.montgomerycountymd.gov/DEP/contact/noise.html): 65 dBA by day and 55 dBA at night.
- [Denver](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Public-Health-Environment/Public-Health-Investigations/Healthy-Families-Healthy-Homes/Noise-Program): most residential limits are 55 dB(A) from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. and 50 dB(A) from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m., with 18 listed exemptions such as snow removal and emergency sirens.
- [Fort Worth](https://www.fortworthtexas.gov/files/assets/public/v/2/communications/documents/city-council-presentations/2024/04-02-2024/update-on-noise-ordinance.pdf): 70 dBA by day and 60 dBA at night, measured for 30 seconds at the complainant's property line, plus a separate "unreasonable noise" subsection that needs no decibel reading.
- [Washington, DC](https://dob.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dob/Noise%20Regulation%20Handbook.pdf): an older handbook lists 60 dB (7 a.m.–9 p.m.) and 55 dB (9 p.m.–7 a.m.) for residential areas. Treat these numbers as historical, not current law, and confirm with the District.

Do not cite a national or federal limit in your letter. Quote your own local code instead, and note any exemption that might apply.

## What should a noise nuisance complaint template include?

Include the facts a reviewer needs to act, and the specific rule you believe is being violated. The point of the "rule" line is that limits and hours differ by place, as the [Denver](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Public-Health-Environment/Public-Health-Investigations/Healthy-Families-Healthy-Homes/Noise-Program) and [Fort Worth](https://www.fortworthtexas.gov/files/assets/public/v/2/communications/documents/city-council-presentations/2024/04-02-2024/update-on-noise-ordinance.pdf) examples above show, so quote your own code. Adapt this structure:

> **To:** [Office that handles noise complaints, e.g., code enforcement / health department / police non-emergency]
> **From:** [Your name, address, phone, email]
> **Date:** [Date]
> **Re:** Noise complaint at [your address] regarding [source address or description]
>
> I am writing to report ongoing noise that interferes with the use of my home.
>
> **What:** [Describe the noise: music, barking, machinery, parties, etc.]
> **Where:** [Source location, as best you know it]
> **When:** [List dates, start and end times, and how often it recurs]
> **Effect on me:** [Sleep disruption, inability to work or rest, etc.]
> **Steps I have taken:** [Conversations, notes left, prior reports, with dates]
> **Rule I believe applies:** [Cite your local ordinance section and any hours or limits it sets]
>
> I request that you [investigate / measure the noise / enforce the ordinance] and let me know the outcome or next steps.
>
> Sincerely, [Name]

Keep the tone factual. Specific dates and times give an enforcer something to verify; adjectives do not. This article did not find a primary source making a dated log legally required, so treat one as good practice, not a requirement.

## Is a neighbor's noise a legal nuisance I can sue over?

Possibly, but the bar is higher than annoyance. A private nuisance claim turns on [significant harm to your use or enjoyment of your land](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/private_nuisance). That is a civil claim you bring yourself, separate from a city enforcing its noise ordinance. If you are thinking about this route, describe the pattern and its effect clearly in your letter, and check your state's rules before deciding how to proceed.

## Can I blame my landlord for noisy neighbors?

Only sometimes. [The covenant of quiet enjoyment is an implied term in every lease](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/covenant_of_quiet_enjoyment), but a breach generally requires more than minor inconveniences: the landlord must substantially interfere with an essential aspect of the premises. Cornell's example is landlord-run construction whose dust, debris, and noise force a tenant out. Whether a landlord must act on neighbor-to-neighbor noise varies by state, so check your state's landlord-tenant rules.

## Will my complaint lead to a fine?

It depends on the ordinance. In the 60-ordinance review, [45 of 60 had both written enforcement and penalties, while seven (11.7%) had neither](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9347828/). Penalty amounts differ by place. Ask for enforcement under your local code and let the agency apply whatever its ordinance provides.

## Need help putting your complaint into words?

Noise rules differ from place to place, as the [review of 60 community noise ordinances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9347828/) shows, so your letter works best when it names your local rule. If you want a starting point for the wording, you can [draft your letter here](/letters).

## FAQ

**Is there an official noise nuisance complaint form?**
This article did not find a single official national form. Noise rules and complaint channels are set locally, so check your own city or county website for any required form or portal. The [review of 60 U.S. ordinances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9347828/) found enforcement authorities vary widely.

**Do I need a decibel reading to complain?**
Not always. Some local codes set decibel limits, but [Fort Worth's ordinance](https://www.fortworthtexas.gov/files/assets/public/v/2/communications/documents/city-council-presentations/2024/04-02-2024/update-on-noise-ordinance.pdf) has a separate unreasonable-noise subsection that needs no decibel reading. [DC's handbook](https://dob.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/dob/Noise%20Regulation%20Handbook.pdf), an older document that may not reflect current law, also says a nighttime noise disturbance can be found without a measurement.

**Is noise from a neighbor a legal nuisance I can sue over?**
Only if it significantly harms your use and enjoyment of your land, per [Cornell's private nuisance definition](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/private_nuisance). Private nuisance is a civil claim, separate from asking the city to enforce a noise ordinance.

**Can I hold my landlord responsible for noisy neighbors?**
Not automatically. A breach of [quiet enjoyment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/covenant_of_quiet_enjoyment) generally requires more than minor inconvenience, and Cornell's example involves the landlord's own conduct. Landlord duties vary by state.

**Should I put a specific fine or penalty in my letter?**
No. Penalties vary, and [some ordinances have none](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9347828/). Ask for enforcement under your local code instead of citing a penalty you have not verified.
