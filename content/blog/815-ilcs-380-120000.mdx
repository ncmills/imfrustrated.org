# 815 ILCS 380 and the "120,000" Confusion: What Illinois's Lemon Law Actually Says

If you searched for **"815 ILCS 380" and "120,000,"** here's the short answer: there is no "$120,000" in the statute, and the law does not reach a car with **120,000 miles** on it either. 815 ILCS 380 is Illinois's **New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act** — the state Lemon Law — and its coverage window is **1 year or 12,000 miles, whichever comes first.** The "120,000" you typed is almost certainly a stray zero on **12,000 miles.** This post walks through what the law actually covers.

## What is 815 ILCS 380?

815 ILCS 380 is the **New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act**, Illinois's version of a motor-vehicle "Lemon Law." As set out in the definitions at [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm), it is a consumer-protection statute that gives the buyer of a qualifying **new** vehicle the right to a replacement or refund when the manufacturer repeatedly fails to fix a serious defect during an early ownership window. It is not a tax rule, a price cap, or a financing law — which is why no purchase-price threshold appears in it at all.

## Is there a "$120,000" threshold in the statute?

No. There is no dollar figure of any kind in 815 ILCS 380 — no price cap, and no minimum purchase price for a vehicle to qualify. Reading the definitions in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm) alongside the presumptions and remedy in [815 ILCS 380/3](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm), the numeric thresholds that actually appear are a **1-year / 12,000-mile** warranty period, an **8,000-pound** weight ceiling, **4 repair attempts**, and **30 business days** out of service. Illinois's Lemon Law does not turn on how expensive your car was, so treat "120,000" as a typo or autocomplete artifact for **12,000 miles.**

## Does the Illinois Lemon Law cover a car with 120,000 miles?

No — a vehicle at 120,000 miles is far outside the coverage window. The Act defines the **"statutory warranty period"** in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm) as **"the period of one year or 12,000 miles, whichever occurs first after the date of the delivery of a new vehicle."** That's the window in which the repeated-repair presumptions have to be met. So the relevant number for eligibility is **12,000 miles**, not 120,000 — a car that has covered 120,000 miles has long since passed both the one-year mark and the mileage mark. If a defect is showing up that late, the Lemon Law is not your tool; a used-car or warranty claim would be a different analysis.

## What vehicles, buyers, and defects does 815 ILCS 380 cover?

The Act covers a **new vehicle** bought or leased for personal use by a **consumer**, and only reaches defects serious enough to count as a **nonconformity.** Under [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm), a covered new vehicle is a passenger car or a Second Division motor vehicle **weighing 8,000 pounds or less**, plus recreational vehicles (excluding certain camping and travel trailers). A "consumer" is an individual who **purchases — or leases for at least one year — a new vehicle** for primarily personal, household, or family purposes (the definition also reaches certain fire departments). The key word is **new**: the Act protects buyers of new vehicles, not used-car buyers, and not vehicles over 8,000 pounds.

The same section defines what makes a car a "lemon." A [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm) **nonconformity** is a failure to conform to express warranties that **"substantially impairs the use, market value or safety"** of the vehicle. A minor cosmetic rattle usually isn't enough; a defect that makes the car unsafe or meaningfully less valuable is the target. Two facts drive the analysis: the defect must be substantial, and it must have persisted through the manufacturer's repair attempts within the statutory warranty period.

## How many repair attempts or days out of service trigger the presumption?

Illinois presumes a **"reasonable number of attempts"** once the same defect has been in for repair **4 or more times**, or the vehicle has been out of service **30 or more business days.** [815 ILCS 380/3](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm) creates that presumption when, during the statutory warranty period, the **same nonconformity has been subject to repair 4 or more times** and still exists — or when the vehicle has been **out of service for repair a total of 30 or more business days.** Keep every repair order, invoice, and drop-off/pick-up date; those documents are what establish the count.

## What can you get — replacement or refund?

The remedy is a **replacement vehicle or a full refund**, less a use allowance. Under [815 ILCS 380/3](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm), if the seller can't conform the vehicle after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must **either provide a replacement vehicle of like model line (or a comparable vehicle), or refund the full purchase or lease price plus collateral charges, less a reasonable allowance for the consumer's use.** The statute does not publish a per-mile offset formula — it calls for a "reasonable allowance for use," which is worked out case by case.

## Do you have to try the manufacturer's dispute program first?

Sometimes yes. If the manufacturer runs a qualifying informal dispute-settlement program, you must use it before the refund/replacement remedy applies. [815 ILCS 380/4](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K4.htm) provides that when a manufacturer maintains an **informal dispute-settlement procedure that conforms to 16 C.F.R. Part 703**, the consumer must **first resort to that procedure** before the Section 3 remedy kicks in. Check your owner's manual and warranty booklet to see whether your manufacturer has such a program.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is 815 ILCS 380?**
It's the Illinois New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act — the state's motor-vehicle Lemon Law — which lets a buyer of a qualifying new vehicle get a replacement or refund when the manufacturer can't fix a substantial defect. See [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm).

**Does 815 ILCS 380 have a $120,000 price limit?**
No — no dollar amount, price cap, or minimum purchase price appears anywhere in the Act. Eligibility turns on the vehicle type and the 1-year / 12,000-mile window, not price, per [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm).

**Does the Illinois Lemon Law cover a car with 120,000 miles?**
No. Coverage runs during the statutory warranty period — 1 year or 12,000 miles, whichever comes first — so 120,000 miles is far outside it. See [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm).

**How many repair attempts before the Illinois Lemon Law applies?**
The law presumes a reasonable number of attempts once the same defect has been repaired 4 or more times, or the vehicle has been out of service 30 or more business days, during the statutory warranty period. See [815 ILCS 380/3](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm).

**What vehicles are covered?**
Passenger cars and Second Division vehicles weighing 8,000 pounds or less bought or leased for personal, household, or family use, plus certain fire-department and recreational vehicles, as defined in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm). Used vehicles are not covered.
