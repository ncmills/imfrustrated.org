# 815 ILCS 380 and the "120,000" Confusion: What Illinois's Lemon Law Actually Says

If you searched for **"815 ILCS 380" and "120,000,"** here's the short answer: there is no "$120,000" in the statute. 815 ILCS 380 is Illinois's **New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act** — the state Lemon Law — and the only numbers it actually uses are **12,000 miles, 8,000 pounds, 4 repair attempts, 30 business days, and 18 months.** The "120,000" is almost certainly a mix-up with the **12,000-mile** warranty window. This post walks through what the law really covers.

## What is 815 ILCS 380?

815 ILCS 380 is the **New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act**, Illinois's version of a motor-vehicle "Lemon Law." Its short title is set out in [815 ILCS 380/1](https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K1.htm). The Act gives a buyer of a qualifying new vehicle the right to a refund or a replacement when the manufacturer repeatedly fails to fix a serious defect during an early ownership window. It is a consumer-protection statute — not a tax code, a price cap, or a financing rule — which is why no purchase-price threshold like "$120,000" appears in it.

## Is there a "$120,000" threshold in the statute?

No. There is no dollar figure of any kind in 815 ILCS 380. Reading the definitions in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm) alongside the presumptions in [815 ILCS 380/3](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm), the only numeric thresholds in the Act are:

- **1 year or 12,000 miles** — the statutory warranty period.
- **8,000 pounds** — the vehicle-weight ceiling for a covered Second Division vehicle.
- **4 repair attempts** for the same defect.
- **30 business days** out of service for repair.
- **18 months** to file a claim.

If you saw "120,000" attached to this statute, treat it as a typo or an autocomplete artifact for **12,000 miles**. Illinois's Lemon Law does not turn on how expensive your car was.

## What vehicles and buyers does 815 ILCS 380 cover?

The Act covers a **"new vehicle"** bought or leased for personal use, and a **"consumer"** who buys it for personal, household, or family purposes. Under the definitions in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm), a covered new vehicle is a passenger car, a Second Division motor vehicle **weighing under 8,000 pounds**, a vehicle purchased by a fire department or fire protection district, or a recreational vehicle (excluding certain camping and travel trailers). A "consumer" is an individual who purchases — or leases for at least one year — that vehicle for **primarily personal, household, or family purposes.** Used vehicles, motorcycles, and vehicles over 8,000 pounds fall outside these definitions.

## What counts as a "lemon" under the law?

A vehicle qualifies when it has a **nonconformity** the manufacturer can't repair after a reasonable number of tries. [815 ILCS 380/2](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm) defines a nonconformity as a failure to conform to express warranties that **substantially impairs the use, market value, or safety** of the vehicle. A cosmetic rattle usually isn't enough; a defect that makes the car unsafe or meaningfully less valuable is the target. Two facts drive the analysis: the defect must be substantial, and it must have persisted through the manufacturer's repair attempts.

## How many repair attempts or days out of service trigger the presumption?

Illinois presumes a "reasonable number of attempts" once the same defect has been in for repair **4 or more times**, or the vehicle has been out of service **30 or more business days.** [815 ILCS 380/3](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm) creates a rebuttable presumption when, during the statutory warranty period, the **same nonconformity has been subject to repair 4 or more times** and still isn't fixed — or when the vehicle has been **out of service for repair for a total of 30 or more business days.** Keep every repair order, invoice, and drop-off/pick-up date; those documents are what establish the count.

## What can you get — refund or replacement?

The remedy is a **replacement vehicle or a full refund**, less a use allowance. Under [815 ILCS 380/3](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm), the manufacturer must, at the consumer's option, either replace the vehicle or **refund the full purchase price or lease cost — including all collateral charges — minus a reasonable allowance for the consumer's use.** The statute does not publish a mileage-offset formula; it simply calls for a "reasonable allowance for use," which is negotiated or decided case by case.

## Do you have to arbitrate first?

Sometimes yes. If the manufacturer runs a qualifying informal dispute-settlement program, you must use it before the refund/replacement remedy kicks in. [815 ILCS 380/4](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K4.htm) provides that when a manufacturer maintains an **informal dispute-settlement procedure that substantially complies with 16 C.F.R. Part 703**, the consumer must first resort to that procedure before the Section 3 remedy applies. Check your owner's manual and warranty booklet to see whether your manufacturer has such a program.

## How long do you have to act, and what does the dealer owe you?

You have **18 months from original delivery** to file, and the seller must hand you a written statement of your rights. [815 ILCS 380/6](https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K6.htm) requires that any action under the Act be **commenced within eighteen (18) months following the date of original delivery** of the vehicle to you. Separately, [815 ILCS 380/7](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K7.htm) requires the seller, at delivery, to give the consumer a **clear and conspicuous written statement** of the rights and presumptions under Section 3. One strategic note: under [815 ILCS 380/5](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K5.htm), a consumer who elects to proceed and settle under this Act is **barred from a separate cause of action under the Uniform Commercial Code**, so choose your track deliberately.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is 815 ILCS 380?**
It's the Illinois New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act — the state's motor-vehicle Lemon Law — which lets a buyer of a qualifying new vehicle get a refund or replacement when the manufacturer can't fix a substantial defect. See [815 ILCS 380/1](https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K1.htm).

**Does 815 ILCS 380 have a $120,000 price limit?**
No — no dollar amount appears anywhere in the Act. The "120,000" is almost always a mix-up with the 12,000-mile warranty period in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm).

**How many repair attempts before the Illinois Lemon Law applies?**
The law presumes enough attempts once the same defect has been repaired 4 or more times, or the vehicle has been out of service 30 or more business days, within 1 year or 12,000 miles. See [815 ILCS 380/3](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K3.htm).

**How long do I have to file an Illinois Lemon Law claim?**
You must commence the action within 18 months of the vehicle's original delivery date, per [815 ILCS 380/6](https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K6.htm).

**What vehicles are covered?**
Passenger cars and Second Division vehicles under 8,000 pounds bought or leased for personal, household, or family use, plus certain fire-department and recreational vehicles, as defined in [815 ILCS 380/2](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081503800K2.htm).
