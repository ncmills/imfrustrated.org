*This article is general information, not legal advice. Noise rules differ by city and county — confirm your local ordinance before relying on any number here.*

## What decibel level makes noise a legal problem?

There's no single number that applies everywhere — what counts as "too loud" is set locally, not federally. The EPA's 1974 protective-levels document recommends an average 24-hour residential exposure limit of [55 decibels outdoors and 45 decibels indoors](https://www.nonoise.org/library/levels74/levels74.htm) to prevent activity interference and annoyance, with a separate 70 decibel ceiling identified as the level that prevents measurable hearing loss over a lifetime. But these are health-based recommendations, not binding law enforceable against a neighbor. Actual enforceable limits come from your city or county: New York City prohibits residential noise that exceeds the ambient sound level by [7 dB(A) at night or 10 dB(A) during the day](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/ViewReport.ashx?M=R&N=Master&GID=61&ID=442539&GUID=E2A5A615-33B0-4B08-9C56-CD79CEA96A87&Extra=WithText&Title=Legislation+Details+%28With+Text%29), while Montgomery County, Maryland sets flat caps of [65 dBA daytime and 55 dBA nighttime](https://www.montgomerycountymd.gov/DEP/contact/noise.html) at the nearest residential property line. Before writing a complaint letter, look up your own jurisdiction's number — citing the wrong standard weakens your letter.

## Does the time of day change the rules?

Usually yes — most ordinances with numeric limits cut the allowable level at night. In unincorporated King County, Washington, the residential-to-residential nighttime limit (10pm–7am weekdays) is [45 dB(A), ten decibels below the 55 dB(A) daytime limit](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dph/health-safety/environmental-health/report-noise-complaint). New Jersey's statewide standard for stationary commercial and industrial sources similarly drops from [65 decibels during the day to 50 decibels at night](https://www.nj.gov/dep/enforcement/NoiseOrdinancevsNuisanceCodeAug08.pdf) under the Noise Control Act of 1971. There is no universal "quiet hours" window that applies everywhere — the 10pm–7am pattern shows up repeatedly but is set locally, so check your own ordinance for the exact hours before you cite a time in your letter.

## What should a neighbor noise complaint letter actually say?

A good letter documents the problem, cites your local limit, and proposes a fix — no fixed format is required, since no government agency mandates specific wording for a neighbor-to-neighbor complaint. An effective letter still does a few specific things: states the problem plainly, documents when it happens, references the applicable local limit, and proposes a resolution before escalation. A workable structure:

1. **Date and address** — yours and theirs.
2. **The specific noise** — source (music, barking, construction, equipment), approximate times, and frequency over the past weeks.
3. **The applicable rule, if you know it** — for example, noting that your city's ordinance sets a nighttime limit, similar to how [NYC caps amplified music at 42 decibels measured inside nearby residences](https://www.nyc.gov/html/misc/pdf/noise_pamphlet.pdf), or how your county applies a day/night differential like [Montgomery County's 65/55 dBA split](https://www.montgomerycountymd.gov/DEP/contact/noise.html).
4. **What you're asking for** — a specific, reasonable change (earlier cutoff time, lower volume, rescheduling work).
5. **A statement that you'll escalate to local authorities or code enforcement if it continues.**

Keep it factual and non-accusatory — it's meant to document the problem and open dialogue, not to threaten. If you want a structured starting point rather than building one from scratch, our [letter templates](/letters) include a neighbor noise complaint format you can adapt to your facts.

## Who do I report a noise complaint to if the letter doesn't work?

It depends entirely on your city or county — there's no single national hotline. In New York City, all noise complaints go through [311](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dep/downloads/pdf/air/noise/noise-code-guide-summary.pdf), which routes them to the relevant enforcement agency. Other jurisdictions split responsibility by noise source: King County, Washington directs certain complaints to the [sheriff's office or a permitting division](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dph/health-safety/environmental-health/report-noise-complaint) depending on what's causing the noise, rather than a single central number. Before you escalate past a letter, find your specific jurisdiction's noise-complaint reporting channel — it's usually listed on the city or county government website alongside the ordinance itself.

## Can I take a neighbor to court over noise instead of just writing a letter?

In many states, a persistent noise nuisance can be pursued in small claims court for monetary damages, but there's no standardized outcome or guaranteed result — small claims limits, procedures, and success vary by state and aren't governed by a single federal rule. What is consistently documented is the local decibel standard you'd point to as evidence of a violation — for example, New Jersey's statewide stationary-source limits of [65 decibels day and 50 decibels at night](https://www.nj.gov/dep/enforcement/NoiseOrdinancevsNuisanceCodeAug08.pdf), or a comparable local ordinance where you live. A written complaint letter is typically treated as the sensible first step, since it creates a dated, factual record showing you raised the issue before any court filing. If the noise continues after a documented letter and a call to local code enforcement, that paper trail is often what a small claims court will want to see first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What decibel level counts as a noise violation?**
It depends on where you live. EPA guidance recommends [55 dBA outdoors and 45 dBA indoors](https://www.nonoise.org/library/levels74/levels74.htm) as health-protective thresholds, but enforceable limits are set locally — compare NYC's ambient-plus-7-to-10-dB(A) rule to Montgomery County's flat [65/55 dBA day/night caps](https://www.montgomerycountymd.gov/DEP/contact/noise.html).

**Does noise have to be measured differently at night?**
Yes, in most ordinances with numeric limits. [King County cuts its daytime 55 dB(A) limit to 45 dB(A) at night](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dph/health-safety/environmental-health/report-noise-complaint), and New Jersey's statewide standard drops from [65 to 50 decibels overnight](https://www.nj.gov/dep/enforcement/NoiseOrdinancevsNuisanceCodeAug08.pdf).

**Can I sue my neighbor for noise?**
Many states allow a noise nuisance claim in small claims court, but no specific outcome or dollar amount is guaranteed — it varies by state. A documented complaint letter is the typical first step.

**Who do I report a noise complaint to?**
It varies by location. NYC residents call [311](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dep/downloads/pdf/air/noise/noise-code-guide-summary.pdf); other jurisdictions route noise complaints to a sheriff's office or permitting division depending on the source.

**Is there a required format for a noise complaint letter?**
No — no government source mandates specific wording or content. It's a practical documentation tool, not a legal filing requirement.
