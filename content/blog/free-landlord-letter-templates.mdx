*This is general information, not legal advice. Rules vary by state and city, so confirm the details for your situation with your local housing agency or a licensed attorney.*

A good landlord letter is short, dated, specific, and asks for one clear thing by a clear date. Below are the letters tenants most often need, what to put in each, and which pieces are backed by official sources and which are simply good practice.

## Do I need to ask my landlord for repairs in writing?

Writing it down is the safest move, because it gives you a dated record. In HUD-assisted housing, residents have the right to [have repairs performed in a timely manner upon request](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/doc_12162.pdf), and a written request makes that "upon request" easy to prove.

NYC's housing agency gives practical instructions for tenants who get no response: [write a letter describing the problems and requesting repairs by a certain date](https://www.nyc.gov/site/hpd/services-and-information/tenants-rights-and-responsibilities.page), send it by certified mail to the owner and the management company, and keep a copy.

A repair letter should include:

- Your name, address, and unit number
- The date
- Each problem, described plainly (what is broken, where, and since when)
- The date by which you want the repair done
- A note that you are keeping a copy

We did not find a federal repair deadline for private rentals, so the date you request is a reasonable ask, not a legal countdown. Check your state's rules before treating it as one.

## What should a notice of noncompliance say?

It should state which lease term or legal duty the landlord has not met, and what you want done. State law often gives such a notice real legal weight, so the details matter.

Florida is one documented example. There, a tenant must [give the landlord written notice of noncompliance with the rental agreement or state law](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-resources/landlord-tenant-law-in-florida), and the tenant may terminate the rental agreement if the landlord does not comply within seven days of delivery.

Do not assume your state works the same way. We found no primary source for lease termination, rent withholding, or notice periods outside Florida, so look up your own state's tenant guide before you threaten any of those steps in a letter. If the stakes are high, such as leaving the unit, talk to a tenant attorney or legal aid office first.

## Can I use a letter to request a disability accommodation?

Yes. The Fair Housing Act bars discrimination in rental because of a handicap of the renter or of any person associated with that renter, under [42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(1)–(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604). It also treats a refusal of reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when necessary for equal use and enjoyment of the home, as discrimination under § 3604(f)(3)(B), a point discussed in the [HUD/DOJ joint statement](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/reasonable_modifications_mar08.pdf), which only partly confirms it, so check the statute text itself.

A letter should say:

- That you (or a household member) have a disability, without needing to over-explain
- The specific change you are asking for (a rule exception, a policy change, a service)
- Why it is needed so you can use and enjoy the home equally

If you need a physical change to the unit, that is a separate category. HUD and DOJ define a reasonable modification as [a structural change to existing premises, occupied or to be occupied by a person with a disability, that gives the person full enjoyment of the premises](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/reasonable_modifications_mar08.pdf).

We could not confirm from a primary source whether a request must be in writing, or what documentation a landlord may ask for. Putting it in writing is still wise for your records. For documentation questions, check HUD's guidance directly.

## What if a landlord denies me because of a background check?

Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, a landlord who uses a tenant background check against you may owe you an "adverse action" notice. The requirement is not limited to outright denials. It also covers [raising the rent or security deposit, or requiring a co-signer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/federal-housing-agencies-strongly-encourage-landlords-to-provide-tenants-written-notice-of-their-rights/), and federal agencies say written notice is the best way to meet it.

We could not verify the notice's required contents (such as screening-company contact details, dispute rights, and free-report timing) against a live primary source, so we are not listing them here. Check the current guidance on ftc.gov or consumerfinance.gov. Note also that the CFPB page above dates from 2021 and sits alongside pandemic-era material, so confirm it still says what is described here.

If you were denied and received no notice, a short letter to the landlord asking which screening company, if any, was used is a reasonable first step.

## How fast must a landlord return a deposit or respond to a notice?

It depends on the state, and there is no single federal deadline we can point to for private rentals. Florida shows how specific these rules get: if the landlord does not intend to claim any of the deposit, it [must be returned within 15 days of the tenant vacating](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-resources/landlord-tenant-law-in-florida), and a longer notice window applies if the landlord intends to claim part of it.

Florida also requires a landlord to serve written notice allowing [three days, excluding weekends and legal holidays, to pay rent or vacate](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-resources/landlord-tenant-law-in-florida) before starting legal action to evict.

A deposit letter should include your forwarding address, your move-out date, and a request for the deposit or an itemized explanation of any deductions. Send it in a way that leaves a record, and keep a copy.

## Should I send a template as-is?

No. Treat any template as a skeleton. Fill in real dates, real facts, and one specific request, and remove anything you cannot back up. No primary source we found says a landlord must accept a letter in a particular format, so check whether your lease or local agency requires a specific delivery method. The [HUD resident rights document](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/doc_12162.pdf) is a useful reference if you live in HUD-assisted housing.

## FAQ

**Is a template letter legally sufficient on its own?**
No source we found says a template is automatically sufficient, or that a landlord must accept any particular format. A template is a starting point: fill in your dates, facts, and requested action, and check your state or city housing agency for any required form or delivery method. See the [HUD resident rights document](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/doc_12162.pdf) for rights in HUD-assisted housing.

**How should I send a repair request?**
NYC's housing agency tells tenants who get no response to write a letter describing the problems and [requesting repairs by a certain date](https://www.nyc.gov/site/hpd/services-and-information/tenants-rights-and-responsibilities.page), send it by certified mail to the owner and management company, and keep a copy. That is a sound habit anywhere, even though NYC's guidance is local.

**How long does my landlord have to return my deposit?**
It depends on the state. In Florida, the landlord must return the deposit [within 15 days](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-resources/landlord-tenant-law-in-florida) of move-out if it does not intend to claim any of it. We found no single federal deadline for private rentals, so check your own state's rules.

**Where do I complain about housing discrimination?**
You can file a fair housing complaint with HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity through the online form at hud.gov. The [HUD/DOJ joint statement](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/reasonable_modifications_mar08.pdf) also lists a mailing address, but it is a 2008 document, so confirm current details on hud.gov before mailing anything.

**What if the landlord denies my application because of a background check?**
The Fair Credit Reporting Act's adverse action requirement covers [denying an application, raising the rent or security deposit, or requiring a co-signer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/federal-housing-agencies-strongly-encourage-landlords-to-provide-tenants-written-notice-of-their-rights/), and federal agencies say written notice is the best way to meet it.
