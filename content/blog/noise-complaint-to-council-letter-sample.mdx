*This article is general information, not legal advice. Noise rules, quiet hours, and complaint procedures are set locally, so confirm the details with your own city or county.*

## Who do I write to about a noise problem?

Write to your local government, because that is where noise control is primarily handled. Congress stated that [primary responsibility for control of noise rests with State and local governments](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title42/html/USCODE-2021-title42-chap65-sec4901.htm), with federal action essential mainly for major noise sources in commerce. Check your city or county website to find which office takes noise complaints, and reference your local rules in your letter rather than a federal statute.

This article could not confirm any specific city's quiet hours, decibel limits, or complaint process from a government source. Look those up on your own council's website and quote them directly in your letter.

## Does a federal noise law give me the right to complain?

No. The Noise Control Act of 1972 is codified at [42 U.S.C. 4901–4918](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/COMPS-888/pdf/COMPS-888.pdf), and its stated purposes are to coordinate federal noise research, authorize federal noise emission standards for products in commerce, and give the public information about products' noise characteristics. [EPA's archived summary](https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/laws-regulations/summary-noise-control-act_.html) describes the Act's national policy as promoting an environment free from noise that jeopardizes health and welfare. The Act does not create a general federal right to complain about neighbor or street noise, so cite your local ordinance instead.

## Can I cite decibel numbers like 55, 45, or 85 in my letter?

Be careful. These figures come from other contexts and are not neighbor-noise limits.

- **HUD (55 and 45 dB):** HUD's goal is an exterior day-night average sound level of [no more than 55 decibels, and an interior level of no more than 45 decibels](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/51.101). These are goals for HUD program purposes. HUD also says the levels EPA recommends are not standards and do not take cost or feasibility into account.
- **HUD (65 dB):** under HUD's rules, [sites at or below 65 DNL are "acceptable" for HUD program purposes](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/subtitle-A/part-51/subpart-B). That is a housing-program siting standard, not a limit on what a neighbor may do.
- **NIOSH (85 dBA):** [NIOSH defines noise as hazardous at 85 dBA or higher](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/index.html), with a recommended exposure limit of 85 dBA averaged over an eight-hour workday. For each 3 dBA increase, [NIOSH recommends cutting daily exposure time in half](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/noise/about/noise.html). This is an occupational benchmark, not a residential one.

If you mention any of these, label them accurately, or leave them out and rely on your local ordinance and your own log of incidents.

## What does DNL mean if I see it in a report?

DNL stands for day-night average sound level. [HUD defines it as the 24-hour average sound level, with 10 decibels added to nighttime sounds starting at 10 p.m.](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/subtitle-A/part-51/subpart-B) You do not need to use this term in a council letter unless a report you are quoting does.

## What should a noise complaint letter to the council include?

Keep it short, factual, and specific. The federal Noise Control Act leaves primary responsibility for noise control to [State and local governments](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title42/html/USCODE-2021-title42-chap65-sec4901.htm), so the most useful letters point to your local rules. Include:

1. Your name, address, and contact details.
2. The address or location of the noise source.
3. A dated log: dates, start and end times, and what you heard.
4. How it affects you (sleep, work, children, health), described plainly.
5. What you have already tried, such as speaking to the neighbor or contacting the landlord.
6. The local ordinance or quiet-hours rule you believe applies, quoted from your council's website.
7. A specific request, such as an inspection, a warning letter, or a named contact.

## What does a noise complaint letter to the council look like?

Here is a general-purpose sample you can adapt. Because [noise control is primarily a State and local responsibility](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title42/html/USCODE-2021-title42-chap65-sec4901.htm), fill in the bracketed ordinance reference from your own council's website.

> [Your name]
> [Your address]
> [Phone / email]
> [Date]
>
> To: [Council or department name], [Council address or email]
>
> Re: Noise complaint regarding [address or location of noise source]
>
> Dear [Councillor / Sir or Madam],
>
> I am writing to report ongoing noise from [source, e.g., a neighboring property, a business, a construction site] at [address].
>
> Over the past [period], I have recorded the following incidents: [date, start–end time, description of noise]. A copy of my log is attached.
>
> This noise affects me by [brief, factual description, e.g., disturbing sleep on weeknights].
>
> I have already [spoken with the neighbor on (date) / contacted the landlord on (date)], but the problem has continued.
>
> I understand that [name of local ordinance or rule, quoted from your council's website] may apply. I would be grateful if the council could [investigate / issue a warning / advise me of next steps] and let me know who is handling this complaint.
>
> Thank you for your time. I can be reached at [contact details].
>
> Yours sincerely,
> [Your name]

This article did not find any federal or state law that guarantees a response deadline, a fine amount, or an enforcement outcome, so avoid promising or demanding one in your letter. Ask for acknowledgment and next steps instead.
